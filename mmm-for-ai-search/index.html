Can marketing mix models measure AI search? What the exposure series needs first

Finance teams fund TV and podcasts on the strength of a marketing mix model's estimate. AI answers also move buyers who never click, so the same model is the natural place to look for AI search.

Updated 2026-11-11

Questions this guide answers

  • What is a marketing mix model?
  • Can marketing mix models measure AI search?
  • What data does an MMM need to measure AI search?
  • How can I test whether my AEO changes worked?

Direct answer

Yes, in time. A marketing mix model (MMM) estimates how much each channel contributed to sales from a few years of weekly history, without tracking clicks, which suits AI answers that influence buyers who often never click. Google's Meridian and Meta's Robyn, 2 widely used open-source MMM tools, both have a place for unpaid activity, which is where AI visibility fits.

The catch is the input: a weekly exposure series, meaning how often AI answers show your brand. It needs a method that stays the same over time, enough answers each week that random noise doesn't swamp it, separation from the demand that also drives sales, and history. Robyn's guide asks for "a minimum of two years of historical weekly data," and every AI exposure report from Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI started in 2026.

So start the series now, from a fixed set of unbranded buyer questions asked daily on each engine, and save the platform reports alongside it. While it grows, use page-level or prompt-level experiments to test whether your changes move AI visibility; they need weeks, not years.

What a marketing mix model is

An MMM is a statistical model fitted to your own history. Week by week, you give it how much you spent on each channel or how much exposure each one had, outside factors (controls) such as season, price, and overall category demand, and the sales or leads you got. It estimates how much of the outcome each channel explains, and what more or less spend would do.

Two assumptions shape the estimate. Effects carry over, so a week of exposure keeps working for a while afterward, and returns diminish, so each extra unit adds less than the one before. Meridian calls these Adstock and Hill effects. Because the model works from weekly totals, it needs no user tracking and no clicks.

The price is data. The model can only tell channels apart if their levels moved differently over time, and it needs a lot of history: Google researchers note that "three years of national weekly data is only 156 data points" for a model that often has 20 or more channels.

Why click reports miss AI search

When an AI answer names you and the buyer searches your name later, analytics records a branded search or a direct visit, not AI, so click-based reports see only part of AI's effect, as the web traffic attribution guide explains.

An MMM sits above the attribution stack rather than replacing it: referrals, surveys, and buyer interviews show that AI influence exists, and the model estimates its size.

How AI search goes into the model

In both tools, AI visibility fits the slot for unpaid channels. Meridian calls it organic media, "media activities that have no direct cost," and Robyn calls it organic variables, listing "SEO improvements" among its examples.

With no spend attached, the model reports a contribution to sales, not a return; add your content and staff costs to get one for your ROI business case. The harder part is the exposure series, which needs 4 things.

Platform counts or a fixed question panel

Two kinds of exposure series exist. Platform counts come from the platforms: Search Console counts impressions of your links in Google's AI features, Bing counts AI citations, and OpenAI's Business Hub counts answers that reference an online business's website. They measure real users, but each covers 1 platform, counts links rather than brand names, and rises and falls with how many people ask.

That last point is a trap Google's MMM researchers describe for paid search: ads follow searches, and searches follow demand that also drives sales (Chan and Perry), so a naive model "leads to over-estimates of ROAS," the return on ad spend (Chen et al.). Meridian's remedy is to add search volume as a control. Platform counts need the same.

A fixed question panel asks the same questions on each engine every day, so it doesn't move with demand, and it records brands named in the text, not only links. It measures how often you appear per question, not how many people saw you, so treat it as a visibility index rather than exposure. Weight each question by its share of demand and keep the weights fixed; Meridian rescales each series by its median, so the index doesn't need absolute volumes. What it can't show is a change in how many people ask, which is why the platform counts belong alongside it.

The panel also needs many answers, because answers change across days: in a 2026 study of Google AI Overviews on trending queries, when a query showed an AI Overview on 2 different days, the 2 answers cited exactly the same set of URLs only 0.24% of the time.

The harder problem: AI visibility follows demand too

A fixed panel removes one bias but not the deeper one. Engines mention brands that have more coverage, more reviews, and more people talking about them. A brand that grows for other reasons, such as a launch or a TV campaign, can also gain AI mentions, and a model that doesn't separate the two credits AI search with demand it reflected rather than created.

Meridian's documentation describes the same pattern for search volume: TV and other channels drive branded searches, so branded search volume can sit between them and sales, and Meridian's full-funnel design models it as a result of those channels before using it as a cause; AI visibility can be modeled the same way. Also build the series from unbranded questions, because questions that already name you mostly show demand you had, as branded search does.

Experiments that work while the series grows

Experiments, also called incrementality tests, answer a narrower question than an MMM: did a specific change move AI visibility, or the branded search and leads that follow it. Regional holdout tests rarely fit, because a change to your site reaches every market at once.

  • Page-level split tests. Randomly split comparable pages, change only one group, and compare citations and AI impressions by page. Pinterest published this design for SEO: it splits pages, not users.
  • Prompt-level rollouts. Randomly choose which buyer questions get new content first, and compare their mention rates with the rest over many runs.
  • Untouched controls. Track questions you didn't work on, and 2 or 3 competitors on the same questions. If everything moves together, the engine moved, as performance-based contracts also check.
  • Time-based tests. When a change is global, compare branded search or leads after it with a forecast of what would have happened without it, built from series the change didn't touch. That is the approach behind Google's CausalImpact.

FAQ

Is AI share of voice a good input for an MMM?

Not on its own. Share of voice divides your mentions by everyone's, so it rises when a competitor drops out even if buyers saw you no more often. Build the exposure series from your own mention rate on each question, and keep share of voice as a competitive read.
