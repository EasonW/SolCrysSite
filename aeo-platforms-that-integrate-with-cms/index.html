Direct answer

As of September 2026, the AEO platforms with documented, native CMS publishing are Profound and AirOps. Profound's Agents can create and update content in about 10 CMSs, including WordPress, Webflow, Contentful, Sanity, Drupal, and Adobe Experience Manager, as drafts or published items, and Profound says every Agent run includes an approval step before anything publishes. AirOps publishes into 13 CMSs, including WordPress, Webflow, Contentful, Sanity, HubSpot CMS, and Shopify, with a selectable draft or live status and human review as an optional workflow step. Webflow AEO is the CMS-native option: its agents draft inside Webflow, for Webflow Enterprise sites only.

Adobe Brand Visibility (formerly Adobe LLM Optimizer) and Scrunch mostly skip the CMS and serve changed content to AI agents at the CDN edge. Conductor, Peec AI, Otterly.AI, and Ahrefs Brand Radar stop at recommendations, briefs, or drafts; publishing is yours.

SolCrys does not write to any CMS. It hands governed, human-approved work to the team that ships it: each fix is an Action Hub task with its evidence, a brief document in Google Drive or a linked doc, and an MCP server that lets Claude, ChatGPT, or Cursor read the task and draft from it. Your team publishes, and SolCrys re-runs the same prompts afterward. If you need a vendor-maintained connector that writes into your CMS, SolCrys is not that product.

What "integration" actually means in AEO

"Integrates with your CMS" is one of the most overloaded claims in the AEO buyer cycle. At least 4 different things hide behind the phrase, and they differ in what the platform can touch and who stays accountable for what ships. Calibrate on the vocabulary before you read the matrix.

Export. The platform produces a recommendation, such as "add a direct answer under this H2" or "fix the canonical tag," and hands it to a person as a PDF, a CSV, a Slack message, or a document. The CMS is never touched by the platform. It's a handoff, not an integration, and it can be the right design when the handoff is structured and tracked.

Push. The platform writes into the CMS through an API or connector, usually as a draft for an editor to publish, sometimes as a direct publish. It saves the copy-and-paste step. Whether it also reads the current page before proposing a change, and re-measures after the change goes live, varies by vendor.

Edge delivery. Instead of editing your pages, the platform serves an AI-optimized version of content to AI crawlers at the CDN or edge layer. It changes what crawlers receive without changing your CMS, which is a different risk and governance question from editing canonical pages.

Agent handoff. The platform exposes its findings to an AI assistant your team runs, through MCP or an API, and your assistant does the writing with your own permissions. The platform never holds write access to your CMS; your agent does.

If a vendor says "we integrate with WordPress" on a discovery call, the next question is: which of those 4, into which fields, and who approves before anything goes live?

What a CMS write path buys you, and what it doesn't

A write path removes one step: copying an approved change from the AEO tool into the CMS. For high-volume, low-risk edits, such as meta descriptions, alt text, or structured data that matches what's on the page, that step is real friction, and a push integration earns its keep.

It doesn't remove the 2 steps that decide whether AEO work is safe and useful. The first is approval: someone accountable for the brand has to read what goes live, whether the words arrive by connector or by paste. The second is verification: after the change ships, the same prompts have to be re-run on the same engines before anyone can say the answer moved, and even then the before-and-after is an observation, not proof of cause. A connector that publishes quickly but can't show either step is automation of the easiest part of the job.

Where a write path adds risk is brand-bearing content: product claims, comparisons, pricing language, and anything regulated. For that work, the useful question is not "can it publish?" but "what does the reviewer see before it does, and what record does it keep?"

How SolCrys fits: governed handoff, not CMS writes

SolCrys has no write access to your CMS or your site, and we don't plan to pretend otherwise on this page. What it does is make the handoff to whoever ships (a writer, a web team, or an AI assistant your team runs) structured, approved, and measurable.

The handoff has 3 parts. The task: every recommendation from a Deep Analysis, a Content Audit, or a Signals brief becomes an Action Hub task with its evidence, an owner, a due date, and a named reviewer. The document: with Google Drive connected, starting a content task creates a brief document in Drive, or your team links any document URL, such as a Google Doc or a Notion page, so drafting happens where your writers already work. The assistant: the SolCrys MCP server lets Claude, ChatGPT, or Cursor read the task, its evidence, the Content Audit detail, and your Corporate Context, and our open-source Skills turn that into a draft or an apply-ready page fix.

If your team wants its own agent to write to your CMS, that agent uses your CMS's API with your credentials and your approval rules; it isn't SolCrys writing. SolCrys's part is the governed input and the record. The MCP server's write for this workflow marks an approved or in-progress Deep Analysis task published with its live URL, and only if an admin grants that opt-in permission. It updates the task in SolCrys, never your site. MCP itself is an open protocol, created by Anthropic and donated to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025, so the same server works across clients.

After your team publishes, SolCrys re-runs the same tracked prompts on the same engines and shows, per engine, whether answers start citing the page; a page fixed from a Content Audit is re-audited so you can see which checks recovered. Both are observations, not proof of cause.

The trade is deliberate, and it has a cost. If you want a one-click connector that drafts into WordPress or Contentful, Profound or AirOps will fit better. SolCrys fits when your content lives across several systems, when brand-bearing copy needs a named approver before anyone touches the CMS, or when your team already works through an AI assistant and wants the evidence and the measurement kept in one place.

What to ask vendors about CMS integration

Use these 5 questions to pressure-test any vendor's "we integrate with your CMS" claim. The right answers are short, specific, and willing to be put in writing.

  • Is it export, push, edge delivery, or agent handoff? Ask for the exact data flow: does the platform read the current page from the CMS or from its own crawl, does it write back, as a draft or a live publish, and into which fields?
  • Which CMSs are officially supported, with a documentation link? Officially supported means a documented connector, a maintenance owner, and a changelog. "We can integrate with anything" usually means your engineers will.
  • What does the reviewer see before anything goes live? A platform that can write to your CMS without a named approver, a diff, and a record of who approved what is a liability for brand-bearing content.
  • Can the platform re-run the affected prompts after the change is live? Without that, you ship a fix and never learn whether the answer moved. Ask to see a before-and-after on a real page, and ask how the vendor separates your change from engine-side changes.
  • Is there an open API or MCP server for the parts it doesn't cover? Even if the native CMS list misses your stack, an open surface lets your team or your assistant wire the rest. If the answer is "no API," you're betting the vendor's roadmap reaches your CMS.

Common integration anti-patterns

Four patterns to watch for in vendor demos. All 4 should change your willingness to pay.

A download button called an integration. A CSV or PDF export is a file. That can be fine for an inexpensive monitoring tool. It is not what an enterprise contract labeled "CMS integration" should mean.

Slack notifications without an action path. Posting a message to a channel when something changes is awareness, not execution. It doesn't shorten time-to-fix unless the message leads to an owned, tracked task.

Push without verification. Some tools can write to your CMS but can't re-run the affected prompts afterward. You ship the change and guess, and the team gets false confidence that "the AEO platform handled it."

Connectors that need per-template mapping ceremonies. If onboarding means weeks of mapping every page template to the vendor's data model, the connector is unfinished and the maintenance burden falls on you.

FAQ

Does SolCrys integrate with WordPress or other CMSs?

No. SolCrys has no write access to any CMS. It hands reviewed, evidence-backed work to your team through Action Hub tasks, Google Drive briefs or linked documents, and an MCP server your AI assistant can read. Your team publishes, records the live URL, and SolCrys re-runs the same prompts afterward.

Does Profound integrate with WordPress?

Yes. As of September 2026, Profound Agents can create or update WordPress content with a status such as draft or publish, and Profound says every Agent run includes an approval step before anything publishes. Its CMS list also includes Webflow, Contentful, Sanity, Drupal, and Adobe Experience Manager content fragments.

Does my CMS need an open API to integrate with an AEO platform?

For any write path, yes: the platform, or your own agent, needs an interface to read and write through. Most modern CMSs, including WordPress, Webflow, Contentful, Sanity, HubSpot CMS, and Shopify, ship usable APIs. Older enterprise CMSs and custom-built systems usually fall back to file drops or human publishing.

What about headless CMSs?

Headless CMSs such as Contentful, Sanity, Strapi, and Hygraph are the easiest case for writing, because the API-first design removes theme-template guesswork. The hard part is the front end: the platform needs to know which rendered URL a CMS entry maps to, which usually takes a route map or a framework plugin that exposes it. Without that, re-measuring the right URL after publish gets unreliable.

Can I integrate via Zapier or Make?

For simple flows, yes. Zapier or Make can sit between an AEO platform's API or notifications and your CMS's publish API. The limit is state: they handle "when X, do Y" well, but they don't hold the recommendation, the approval, the publish, and the re-measurement together. For one-shot pushes they're fine; for a governed loop, keep the task and its evidence in a system that tracks all four.

Will my AEO platform break when my CMS updates its API?

It can. Native connectors break on CMS API changes, and how fast the vendor patches them matters. Before signing a multi-year contract, ask for the last few CMS API changes that affected the connector and how long each fix took, and plan for the gap.

Do I need an AEO platform with a CMS integration if my team is small?

Probably not at first. With 1 or 2 writers and a short priority list, a platform that measures, diagnoses, and tracks tasks, plus a tight review habit, gets most of the value. A write path earns its keep when you're shipping many small, low-risk edits a month and the copy-and-paste step has become the bottleneck.

What's the best AEO option for a Webflow site?

If you're on a Webflow Enterprise site plan, Webflow AEO works inside Webflow: technical agents propose fixes you review before publishing, and with Webflow Analyze for Enterprise, AEO analytics tracks ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity and content agents draft into the Webflow CMS. Outside Enterprise, or if you want measurement independent of your CMS vendor, Profound and AirOps both publish into Webflow, and SolCrys measures and hands off reviewed work while your team publishes in Webflow.
