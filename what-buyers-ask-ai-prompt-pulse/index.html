What Buyers Ask AI: Patterns in 1,012 Buyer-Style Questions Across 16 Industries

Keyword tools tell you what people typed into a search box. They don't show the full questions buyers bring to AI assistants. Prompt Pulse is our attempt to map those questions: a curated set of 1,012 buyer-style questions across 16 industries, drawn from public question sources and scored with a modeled, relative Demand tier and trend. It is not a log of real AI conversations, and its stage mix describes the dataset, not everything people ask AI. Within that scope the patterns are useful: 54% of the questions are Decision-stage and only 10% are "what is" awareness questions, commercial intents (recommendation, comparison, and cost) make up 53%, and the questions trending up skew toward concern, especially about whether AI can replace a specialist. Direct answer

Prompt Pulse is a curated set of 1,012 buyer-style questions across 16 industries (verticals dated June 1–4, 2026), each labeled by buying stage, intent, persona, a relative Demand tier, and a trend direction. The questions come from public question sources and are scored with modeled, relative demand. They are not a sample of real ChatGPT or Perplexity conversations, and the Demand tiers are not query counts.

Within the dataset, the mix is lopsided toward decisions: 53.7% of questions are Decision-stage, 36.1% Consideration, and 10.3% Awareness. That describes a set built around buying, not AI usage overall. Across all consumer ChatGPT use, OpenAI's own researchers found, in conversations sampled through mid-2025, that practical guidance, seeking information, and writing account for about 77% of conversations, with product questions one part of seeking information. What the dataset does show is the shape of buying questions once someone is in a category: most ask the AI to choose, compare, or de-risk, not to define.

That shape matters for content and AEO. The questions where an answer can turn into a shortlist are specific and decision-shaped, and a brand built around "what is" explainers is answering the smallest slice of them. The live data is browsable by industry on the Prompt Pulse hub.

What the dataset is, and what it isn't

Start with the unit of analysis. Traditional keyword research counts the short strings people type into a search box ("crm software," "best smart lock"). Prompt Pulse works with the full natural-language question a buyer would put to an AI assistant, such as "Which CRM is considered number one for mid-market B2B sales teams?", because that sentence, not the keyword, is what ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI surfaces answer.

The current set spans 16 industries: consumer Smart Home, Fintech, the B2B SaaS stack (CRM, HR and payroll, accounting, project management, marketing and email, ecommerce, cybersecurity, and SEO and AEO), and the AI-infrastructure layer (GPUs, inference, MLOps, LLM observability, AI cloud, and AI data and storage). Every question carries five labels: a Demand tier (High, Medium, or Low), a trend (Rising, New, Stable, Cooling, or no label when there isn't enough history to call a direction), an intent, a persona, and a buying stage (Awareness, Consideration, or Decision).

  • It is a curated set of buyer-style questions for each industry, drawn from public question sources and written around needs and use cases rather than the vendors competing for them.
  • It is scored with modeled, relative demand. Tiers and trends are relative within each industry, so a High in LLM Inference is not comparable to a High in CRM.
  • It isn't a log of what people typed into ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google's AI surfaces. Prompt-volume figures from any source outside the AI platforms themselves are modeled estimates, ours included.
  • It isn't a random sample of AI usage. The set was built around buying, so its stage and intent mix describe the set, not buyer behavior overall.

What stays internal, and what changed in this update

What you see publicly is the question text and its labels. The underlying volumes, sources, and scoring weights stay internal. Each vertical is a dated snapshot, so treat its shape as a point in time.

This analysis was updated on Sept. 26, 2026 to match the live hub, which now includes Fintech. The original June 3 version covered 15 industries; the stage shares moved by less than 1 point.

Finding 1: In this set, buying questions ask AI to decide, not to define

Sort all 1,012 questions by buying stage and the distribution sits at the bottom of the funnel.

Read the scope carefully. Because the set was curated around buying, a bottom-heavy mix is partly by design. The useful signal is inside it: when you write out the questions that carry demand in a category, the "what is X" explainer is a small part of the buying conversation. Most are the questions of someone who already knows the category and is comparing options, pressure-testing concerns, and deciding.

Finding 2: Each industry's question set has a different shape

The industries differ in how decision-heavy their questions are and in how much of their demand is moving. The table covers every industry on the hub. Demand tiers are left out on purpose: they are relative within each industry, so counting High-tier questions across rows would compare unlike things.

Three patterns stand out. Project Management (69%) and CRM and sales tech (64%) have the most decision-heavy sets, while Ecommerce Platforms (44%) and LLM Inference and Serving (45%) have the least. Movement is concentrated: Smart Home, Fintech, and Cybersecurity hold 80 of the 146 questions flagged rising or new, and across the whole set more questions were cooling (253) than rising or new. Finding 3: What's heating up: "can AI do this without a specialist, and can I trust it?"

146 of the 1,012 questions are flagged rising or new in this snapshot, and their intent mix differs from the set as a whole: Concern is the largest intent among them (49 of 146, or 34%, versus 18.6% overall). Read together, they show a theme that crosses unrelated industries: buyers asking whether AI and automation now let them do work that used to need a developer, an agency, or a specialist, and whether the AI version can be trusted.

It shows up in SEO ("Can a non-technical marketer realistically do their own SEO without a developer?"), in CRM ("Can I use an AI assistant to build a lightweight internal CRM using existing tools I already have?"), in payroll ("Can AI actually handle payroll end-to-end, or does it still need a human to verify everything?"), in email marketing ("Is using an AI writing tool inside an email platform actually improving open and click rates, or is it just a marketing feature?"), and in security ("Is zero trust security achievable without a large dedicated security team, or is it only practical for enterprises?"). In Smart Home, the rising questions cluster around getting started with automation and around frustrations that push people to switch, such as subscriptions and unreliable apps.

The strategic read: in category after category, the rising edge of these question sets is a skeptical do-it-yourself buyer. If your positioning assumes the buyer needs a specialist to evaluate you, or skips the "does it actually work" question, the questions heating up fastest are the ones you are least prepared to answer. Trend is a directional signal, relative within each industry, so treat the movement as a heat map, not a traffic forecast.

Finding 4: The questions are specific, and they're buyable

The Decision-stage questions aren't vague. They name the buyer, the use case, and often the spec, and they are exactly the prompts where being the AI's recommended option can win or lose a deal. A few examples from the High-demand, Decision-stage questions, as they appear on the hub:

  • CRM & Sales Tech: "Which CRM is considered number one for mid-market B2B sales teams?"
  • CRM & Sales Tech: "What are the hidden costs of enterprise CRM platforms I should budget for beyond the license fee?"
  • Cybersecurity: "What are the best penetration testing companies for a financial services firm with strict compliance requirements?"
  • AI Cloud & Cluster Infrastructure: "Which GPU cloud provider gives the best price-to-performance ratio for AI training in 2026?"
  • AI Data, Storage & Memory: "Which vector database is best for a production RAG pipeline handling millions of documents?"
  • Marketing & Email: "What are the best top-5 automation tools for a lean marketing team that needs to automate email, social, and lead scoring?"

Why this matters for SEO and AEO

Buyers do use AI when they buy, even if a curated dataset can't say how often they ask each question. Forrester's State of Business Buying 2026 found 94% of business buyers use AI during their buying process, and Gartner's May 2026 survey found 45% of B2B buyers used generative AI mainly to gather information on vendors and products. Keyword research and question-level demand answer two different questions, and you now need both: a keyword tool shows the head terms people type into Google; Prompt Pulse shows the kind of full question an AI assistant reasons over when it assembles an answer and a shortlist.

The workflow this enables is straightforward, and it doesn't require any of the schema or markup tricks that don't move AI answers:

  • Use the questions as a starting prompt set, then make it yours. Pull the Decision and Consideration questions in your vertical, add the phrasing your own buyers use in sales calls, and see our guide to building an AI search prompt set.
  • Check, honestly, whether your content answers them at all. Look for real answers to the comparison, cost, and concern questions where buying happens, not just explainers for the Awareness ones.
  • Look at the rising questions and write to the demand that's accelerating, not the demand that peaked last year, remembering that trend is directional.
  • Map intent to format: recommendation and comparison questions want genuinely useful comparison content; concern questions want honest "where this goes wrong" answers; cost questions want real numbers.
  • Then measure whether it worked. The gap between "we published content for this question" and "the AI now mentions us on this question" is the whole game, and it's measurable by rerunning the same prompts on the same engines.

What is SolCrys Prompt Pulse?

The current snapshot holds 1,012 questions across 16 industries. The questions come from public question sources and are scored with modeled, relative demand, so the tiers show where demand concentrates within an industry, not how many times anyone asked. How many questions and industries does it cover?

As published on the hub (verticals dated June 1–4, 2026), 1,012 questions across 16 industries: Smart Home, Fintech, the B2B SaaS stack (CRM, HR and payroll, accounting, project management, marketing and email, ecommerce, cybersecurity, and SEO and AEO), and the AI-infrastructure layer (GPUs and accelerators, LLM inference, MLOps, LLM observability, AI cloud, and AI data and storage). Where does the data come from?

Each industry's questions are drawn from public question sources and scored with modeled, relative demand. What's published is the question text and its labels; the underlying volumes, sources, and scoring weights stay internal. Treat Demand tiers and trends as directional and relative within each industry, not as query counts.

Does Prompt Pulse show what people ask AI in general?

No. It is a curated set of buying questions, so its stage and intent mix describe the set, not AI usage overall. For that broader picture, OpenAI's own study of consumer ChatGPT conversations (NBER Working Paper 34255, September 2025) found practical guidance, seeking information, and writing make up about 77% of conversations. Use Prompt Pulse to see the shape of buying questions within a category, not to size how much of AI usage is about buying.

Is this the same as keyword search volume?

No. Keyword volume counts short strings typed into a search box. Prompt Pulse works with full natural-language questions, and its Demand tiers are modeled and relative within each industry, designed to show shape and movement rather than to be read as a volume meter. The two are complementary: keyword tools for search head terms, Prompt Pulse for the questions buyers bring to AI assistants.

How is asking AI different from a Google search, for marketers?

Two ways that matter here. People ask AI in full sentences, so the useful unit is a question, not a keyword. And when you write out the buying questions in a category, most of them are about deciding: in this dataset, 89.7% of the questions are Consideration or Decision stage. That makes AI answers a place where shortlists get formed, so being the recommended option on decision-shaped questions is the high-value target.

How often is Prompt Pulse updated?

Prompt Pulse is published as dated snapshots, not a live feed. Each industry carries the date its data was pulled; the current industries are dated June 1–4, 2026. Tiers and trends describe demand as of that snapshot, so treat each industry's page as a point-in-time map.
