How smaller brands win AI answers: specific beats famous

Big brands start ahead in AI answers: models know more about well-documented names, and in controlled tests familiarity wins ties. But who gets named depends heavily on what the buyer asks. On specific questions, tied to a role, a workload, an integration, or a constraint, the advantage can shift to the brand that wrote the precise answer down. Smaller brands win AI answers one specific question at a time: choose the questions by who is asking, publish the facts only you have, say them the same way everywhere, and measure each question across runs.

Direct answer

Don't fight a market leader for the broadest question in your category first. Win the specific ones. Language models know more about well-documented brands, and in controlled tests familiarity wins ties. But who an answer names depends heavily on what the buyer asks. Specific questions, tied to a role, a workload, an integration, or a constraint, are where a big brand's general content runs thin and a smaller brand's precise facts can carry the answer.

The playbook is short. Choose questions by who is asking and how far along they are. Publish the facts only you have, and state them the same way everywhere. If your category is new, name it and define it. Get your claims confirmed where engines look. Then measure each question on its own, across several runs, because a category-wide average hides exactly the wins a smaller brand gets first.

Why it matters: the shortlist forms early

In 6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report, a survey of more than 4,000 buyers, 94% of buying groups had ranked their preferred vendors before first contact, and they bought from that early favorite 77% of the time. And half of B2B software buyers (51%) now start their research with an AI chatbot more often than with Google, in G2's March 2026 survey of 1,076 buyers and decision-makers.

Each person in a buying group asks a different question. A smaller brand doesn't need to be the default answer to the broadest one. It needs to be the right answer to the questions its best-fit buyers ask early, so it is on the list when the list forms.

Where big brands start ahead

The head start is real. It shows up in 3 ways, and each has a limit.

  • Models know more about well-documented names. Language models "struggle with less popular factual knowledge," found Mallen and colleagues at ACL 2023. In Kandpal and colleagues' ICML 2023 study, a 176-billion-parameter model's accuracy jumped from 25% to above 55% as the number of relevant training documents rose from about 10 to about 10,000. A brand with years of coverage is a better-documented fact.
  • Familiarity breaks ties. In a 2026 preprint, Chu and Hou had GPT-4o-mini, Claude Sonnet, and Gemini 3 Flash choose between 1 real skincare brand and 9 fictional ones. With identical specs, the real brand won all 670 trials. The authors estimate, by interpolation pooled across the 3 models, that an edge as small as a 7.3% lower price or a 0.075-star higher rating would let a fictional brand win half the time. In this controlled setup, familiarity decided ties, and a small stated fact was enough to break them.
  • Associations lean global. In fill-in-the-blank tests at EMNLP 2024, Kamruzzaman and colleagues found that GPT-4o and 3 open models disproportionately associated global brands with positive attributes. Context weakened the pull: when the prompt included the user's country, GPT-4o chose the local brand 75% of the time.

Where the advantage shifts: the specific question

Fame sets the starting odds. These findings show where it stops deciding the answer.

  • The question decides who gets named. Malthouse and colleagues asked 6 current OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic models for top-5 product recommendations, with web search turned off. Category-only questions revealed "substantial omission of established brands." The authors then took 2 established brands the models never recommended for those questions, Craftsman drills and L.L.Bean hiking jackets, and rewrote the questions to describe a shopper's use case, goals, and constraints. Craftsman then appeared in 35.4% of 240 lists, and L.L.Bean in 5.4%. Their conclusion: describing goals and constraints "changes which brands are retrieved." The study is a September 2026 preprint.
  • When a model knows little, retrieval helps most. Mallen and colleagues also found that retrieval "helps significantly" for less popular entities: a small retrieval-augmented model outperformed GPT-3 davinci-003 on the 4,000 least popular questions. Their entities are not brands, and their models were research models, not answer engines. The pattern still points one way: the less a model knows about a name, the more a search can change its answer.
  • Google's AI answers reach past the top results. Across 55,393 queries, Xu, Iqbal, and Montgomery found that 29.8% of domains cited in Google AI Overviews did not appear on the first page of results, and the 10 most-cited hostnames accounted for 29.7% of citations, against 54.5% of first-page links. Kirsten and colleagues found that on average 53% of the domains AI Overviews consult are not in the top 10 organic results. Google itself says that with AI Overviews, people visit "a greater diversity of websites".
  • Longer questions draw more AI answers. In Pew Research Center's study of 68,879 searches, Google showed an AI summary on 53% of searches of 10 words or more, against 8% of one- or two-word searches. Google's own advice for its AI experiences notes that users are "asking longer and more specific questions" and says to "focus on making unique, non-commodity content."

The details that win

Here are 5 moves, in roughly the order a smaller brand should make them.

1. Choose questions by who is asking

Build your question set around roles and stages, not keyword volume. List the people in your buying group, then the questions each asks on the way to a shortlist: fit with what they already run, integration, compliance, migration effort, total cost, and how you compare with the incumbent. Those are the questions where your detail beats their breadth.

2. Publish the facts only you have

Specs and limits, supported configurations, benchmarks with their method, pricing logic, implementation steps, and named customer results. Write each page so it answers one question directly. Models "rely heavily on the relevance of a website to the query, while largely ignoring stylistic features," found Wan, Wallace, and Klein at ACL 2024. Google's guidance points the same way: it asks whether content provides "original information, reporting, research, or analysis".

3. Say it the same way everywhere

An engine that finds 2 versions of your fact may repeat either one, so make your own pages agree first (run a consistency audit). Your canonical page also counts for more than it used to: since August 2026, official pages and documentation carry more of ChatGPT's citations (AI cites consensus, not authority).

4. Name the category, then define it

If your product creates a category, you can be its first and most precise source. Google describes "data voids", topics with "a limited amount of high quality content," as one cause of AI Overviews errors. A term nobody has published yet is a data void. Fill it yourself: define the term, explain it at the depth each role needs, and use the same words everywhere. The set of sources engines trust for a category narrows as the category matures, so the earliest work counts most (the AI citation window).

5. Get confirmed where engines look

Your own pages state the facts; other sources confirm them. Analyst notes, partner and integration pages, conference talks, customer stories, and community threads all count. Even with web search off, the Malthouse study found recommendation prominence associated with "broader marketplace-visibility signals, particularly search interest and online brand conversation," and "limited evidence" that it followed conventional brand popularity. What people say about you online shapes what models know, and a smaller brand can earn that conversation (how corroboration works).

Measure question by question

A category-wide share of voice is the wrong scoreboard for a smaller brand. The incumbent's breadth dominates the average, and your first wins happen on single questions. Track each question on its own: whether you are named, whether you are described accurately, and which sources the answer cites. A wrong description can cost more than no mention at all (how AI describes your brand).

Then judge change across runs. Answers move: when Kirsten and colleagues repeated the same queries 2 months apart, only 18% of the pages AI Overviews used were the same, against 45% for organic search. Engines also disagree with each other: Uberti-Bona Marin and colleagues found that, on average, the ChatGPT and Gemini interfaces share only 5.4% of cited domains for the same query, so track each engine separately. Rerun the same questions against the same baseline before you call anything a result (a testable playbook).

Where not to spend first

A smaller brand loses time in 4 places.

  • The broadest question in your category. That is where the head start is largest. Earn it later, as corroboration builds.
  • Volume. Publishing more generic pages is a bet the research hasn't backed. A critical survey of 45 GEO studies found that "no reviewed technique shows a stable, longitudinal, cross-platform causal effect on organic discoverability." That applies to every tactic, which is why each move on this page gets measured.
  • Decoration. Adding statistics or quotations for their own sake is contested. In a NeurIPS 2025 benchmark, Puerto and colleagues found most such methods "largely ineffective," and the statistics method lowered rankings in 19 of 24 settings. Facts help when they answer the question.
  • Manipulation. Hidden text, planted instructions, fake reviews, and coordinated posting. We refuse them, even when they would move a number.

What it looks like

NextSilicon, a challenger in one of tech's most crowded categories, started at a 1.9% AI mention rate on HPC and AI infrastructure questions, against incumbents that had spent years building authority. The work focused on factual density, comparison framing, and high-impact claims. Within 45 days its mention rate rose to 7.4% (case study).

Cornelis Networks introduced a new networking category under a term nobody had published. We told one story at 3 depths, from the homepage to the category page to the reference architecture, and turned the questions where Cornelis had no presence into the headings and FAQs of the new pages. In 4 weeks, the mention rate on the category's questions rose 12-fold, the overall AI mention rate rose nearly 7 percentage points, and Cornelis took second place in share of voice for the first time (case study).

FAQ

Can a smaller brand compete with big brands in AI answers?

Yes, starting with specific questions. Big brands start ahead on broad questions because models know more about well-documented names. On questions tied to a role, a workload, an integration, or a constraint, fame settles less, and a smaller brand that published a precise, credible answer can win.

Do ChatGPT and other AI engines favor big brands?

Partly. Models know more about well-documented brands, and in controlled tests familiarity wins ties. But a 2026 study of 6 models, with web search off, found that category-only questions omitted even established brands, and Google AI Overviews cite many domains outside the top search results. What the buyer asks changes who gets named.

Should a smaller company target broad "best" questions in AI search?

Not first. That is where a big brand's head start is largest. Start with the specific questions your best-fit buyers ask on the way to a shortlist, and earn the broad question later as corroboration builds.

What content helps a smaller brand get cited by AI answer engines?

Pages that answer one specific question with facts only you have: specs and limits, supported configurations, benchmarks with their method, pricing logic, implementation steps, and named customer results. State them the same way on every page, and get them confirmed by sources you don't control.

How should a smaller brand measure progress in AI answers?

Question by question, not by a category-wide average. Track whether you are named, whether you are described accurately, and which sources are cited, and rerun the same questions across several runs before you call a change a result.
