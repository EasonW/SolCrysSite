One robots.txt rule to opt out of AI training: what __usage_ai-training does at Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic

On Oct. 30, 2026, Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic each backed one robots.txt rule for AI training: a group for the policy token __usage_ai-training. Microsoft starts on Feb. 1, 2027, Google plans support in 2027, OpenAI says in the coming months, and the draft's author says Anthropic has started implementing it. The rule states a training preference; it doesn't block crawling, and each company's own crawler rules keep working. Google is the case to check: when the rule opts a site out and Google-Extended isn't named, Google plans to read it as a Google-Extended disallow, and Google-Extended also controls grounding in the Gemini app. Microsoft adds a separate page-level grounding preference and stops reading NOARCHIVE and NOCACHE as AI controls after March 1, 2027. If you are fine with AI training, change nothing. If you opt out, decide Google-Extended explicitly, then rerun the prompts you track to confirm your citations held.

Direct answer

__usage_ai-training is a robots.txt policy token for one decision: whether AI companies may train generative models on your content. On Oct. 30, 2026, Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic each backed it: Microsoft and OpenAI say they will honor it, Google plans to, and Anthropic has started implementing it. One group, User-Agent: __usage_ai-training followed by Disallow: /, states a training opt-out to all 4 at once.

It states a preference about use. It doesn't block crawling, and each company's rules for its own crawlers keep working, so search crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, Googlebot, and Bingbot are still governed by their own lines. Google is the case to check. When the token opts a site out and Google-Extended isn't named, Google plans to treat it as a Google-Extended disallow, and Google-Extended also controls grounding in the Gemini app. The shortest opt-out can cost Gemini app citations.

For a brand that wants to be cited: if you are fine with AI training, change nothing, because a file without the token expresses no preference and Microsoft defaults to Allow. If you opt out, name Google-Extended explicitly, leave Bing's new grounding preference on its default, and rerun the prompts you track as each company starts honoring the rule.

What was announced on Oct. 30, 2026

4 companies backed the rule on the same day, on 4 timelines; Anthropic's commitment came from the draft's author on the IETF working group's mailing list. Only Microsoft has given a start date. The rule itself is an individual Internet-Draft submitted to the IETF's AI Preferences working group, not yet a standard; Microsoft calls the working group's progress significant enough that it "merits adoption" while that work continues.

How the rule works

The draft, "Machine-Readable Training Preference Signal," adds no new syntax to robots.txt. The details that matter for a site owner are these.

  • It's an ordinary robots.txt group. The user-agent line names a policy token instead of a crawler. Disallow states a preference against training for those paths and Allow a preference for it, with the usual longest-match rules. The draft's example refuses training everywhere except /press-kit/.
  • No group, no preference. User-Agent: * groups never match the token, so existing wildcard rules aren't read as a training choice. In Kelley's words, a file that doesn't use the mechanism, "as is true of every one published today, expresses no preference at all."
  • It doesn't control crawling. The draft says the token "is not a directive for crawling behavior." Rules for a crawler's own user agent still decide what it fetches, though a crawler that collects only for training may skip pages that refuse training.
  • The specific rule should win. The draft says "Specific expressions SHOULD take precedence over general ones" and lets each company treat its own tokens, such as Google-Extended, as more specific. Google has said how it will apply that; OpenAI says its existing crawler controls "will continue to apply and be respected" and will publish its implementation details later.
  • "Training" has a narrow meaning. The draft uses the working group's definition: "Using an asset to modify the learned parameters of a generative AI model." Models used only for "classification, ranking, or scoring" are outside it, so the rule says nothing about search ranking.

The Google catch: the opt-out can cost Gemini app citations

Google's announcement is specific: "When a website uses this policy token to opt out of training in its robots.txt file (and Google-Extended is not explicitly specified), Google plans to interpret that as if the publisher had disallowed Google-Extended."

Google-Extended isn't only a training control. Google's crawler documentation says it governs training of future Gemini models "and for grounding" in "Gemini Apps and Grounding with Google Search on Vertex AI." Google's announcement adds that "Websites that specifically allow Google-Extended are eligible to appear in responses and receive clicks via grounding in Gemini Apps." Once Google supports the token, a site that adds only the 2-line opt-out refuses Gemini training and, by Google's reading, Gemini app grounding too.

Google Search is not affected. Google says Google-Extended "does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search," and AI Overviews and AI Mode follow Googlebot and Search Console's Search generative AI control, as the UK opt-out guide explains.

There is still no Google setting that refuses Gemini training and keeps Gemini app grounding, and the token doesn't create one. What changes is how the choice gets made: without anyone naming Google-Extended, a generic training opt-out now makes the bundled choice for you. Decide Google-Extended on purpose, using the setups below.

Microsoft: training and grounding become separate choices

Microsoft goes further than the shared token. Its announcement covers content used for training and "to inform generated responses, including web-grounded responses in Bing, Copilot, and Microsoft 365," and it splits that use into 2 independent preferences. Both default to Allow, and setting one doesn't change the other.

  • AI Training. Use the shared token in robots.txt for a site or a path, or set it per page with <meta name="Bingbot" content="ai-training:disallow"> or the header X-Robots-Tag: Bingbot: ai-training:disallow. A page-level setting overrides robots.txt.
  • AI Grounding. Page level only, through the same meta tag or header with ai-grounding:disallow or ai-grounding:disallowextended; Allow is the default. Microsoft: "There is no site-wide robots.txt grounding preference."
  • DisallowExtended keeps a page available to "Microsoft-operated experiences such as AI Mode, AI Overview, Copilot, and Microsoft 365," which are Microsoft's own products despite the names, and excludes "grounding services Microsoft provides to third parties, such as Web IQ and grounding through API access in Foundry." Microsoft launched Web IQ on June 2, 2026 as "a suite of AI-native grounding APIs" for AI systems and agents, "a search engine for AI systems."
  • Bing search is untouched. The preferences "do not change eligibility for traditional Bing search results," and the Bing search syndication API, which "returns links for traditional search scenarios," is not affected by the grounding preference.
  • Use, not access. "These settings govern use of content, not crawl access." An Allow setting doesn't override a robots.txt rule that blocks Bingbot, and a new setting takes effect only when Bing recrawls the page. Microsoft says IndexNow can "increase the chance of a recrawl" without guaranteeing a date; see how to get AI engines to see your updates.

What grounding restrictions cost

Microsoft states the trade plainly: "If you select DisallowExtended or Disallow for grounding, you will see reduced brand visibility in AI experiences." For a brand that wants to be cited, leave grounding on its default. DisallowExtended suits a publisher that wants Microsoft's AI products but not other companies' products built on Microsoft's grounding services. Microsoft doesn't name Web IQ's or Foundry's customers, so a brand can't see in advance which AI products DisallowExtended would remove.

NOARCHIVE and NOCACHE are the AI controls Bing documented in 2023: NOARCHIVE keeps a page out of Copilot answers and training, and NOCACHE limits both to the URL, title, and snippet. Microsoft is retiring them as AI controls on this schedule.

Check for tags nobody chose

For a brand, the retirement mostly helps. If NOARCHIVE sits on your pages by accident, or by a decision nobody remembers, it keeps them out of Copilot answers today, and from March 1, 2027 those pages default to Allow for grounding. Don't wait for that: search your templates, CMS settings, and response headers for noarchive and nocache now, and remove them from pages you want cited.

Before you edit robots.txt

The token makes a training opt-out easy to write and easy to get wrong. Run these checks first.

  • Decide training as a policy, then write it once. One __usage_ai-training group, plus an explicit Google-Extended group, states it to all 4 companies as each honors the token. Company-specific lines such as GPTBot, ClaudeBot, and Applebot-Extended still apply, and Apple and other AI companies haven't announced support for the token, so keep those lines consistent with it.
  • Leave the search crawlers alone. The token doesn't change what OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Googlebot, or Bingbot may crawl, but a crawl block still removes you from answers. AI crawler access lists which bot does what.
  • Read the file you actually serve. If a CDN, plugin, or CMS writes robots.txt for you, fetch /robots.txt from your live site after every change, because that file is what each company reads.
  • Find old tags. Search for noarchive, nocache, and X-Robots-Tag rules before Bing's dates, and find out who added them.
  • Log the date. Record every robots.txt, meta tag, and header change, so the verification step has a before and an after.

Verify: did the change cost you citations?

A preference changes nothing until a company honors it and rereads your pages. Microsoft applies a page's setting when Bing recrawls it, and Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic will each start on their own schedule, so the effect arrives engine by engine. Before a change, capture which of your URLs each engine cites on the prompts you track. After each start date, rerun the same prompts on the same engines and compare.

For the Gemini app, where Google-Extended applies, ask your most important prompts in the app itself before and after Google starts honoring the token. Judge across several runs, because engines change what they cite on their own; how many runs you need covers the noise. For Copilot and Bing's AI summaries, Bing Webmaster Tools' AI Performance report counts citations of your pages; what the free reports show compares it with Search Console and GA4.

FAQ

What is __usage_ai-training?

A robots.txt policy token for AI training preferences, defined in an IETF Internet-Draft published on Oct. 30, 2026. A group that starts User-Agent: __usage_ai-training and says Disallow: / states a preference against training generative AI models on your content; Allow states a preference for it. Microsoft and OpenAI have said they will honor it, Google plans to, and Anthropic has started implementing it. It doesn't block crawling, and it doesn't replace company-specific rules such as GPTBot or Google-Extended.

Does opting out of AI training remove my site from AI answers?

Not by itself at Microsoft, where grounding is a separate setting, and OpenAI and Anthropic describe it as a training signal that sits alongside their existing crawler controls. At Google it can: when Google-Extended isn't named, Google plans to read the opt-out as a Google-Extended disallow, and Google-Extended also controls grounding in the Gemini app. AI Overviews and AI Mode are not affected.

Does the shared AI training opt-out affect Gemini?

It can. Google-Extended covers Gemini training and grounding in Gemini Apps and Grounding with Google Search on Vertex AI, and Google plans to treat a token opt-out as a Google-Extended disallow when Google-Extended isn't named. Adding a User-Agent: Google-Extended group with Allow: / keeps Gemini app grounding, and Gemini training with it.

When will Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic honor the shared AI training signal?

Microsoft from Feb. 1, 2027, as Bing crawls. Google in 2027; its crawler documentation says early 2027. OpenAI "in the coming months," with details to follow. Anthropic has started implementing support, and the draft's author says adding it across Anthropic's services will take time.

What replaces Bing's NOARCHIVE and NOCACHE for AI?

Separate AI Training and AI Grounding preferences. From Feb. 1, 2027, Bing honors the shared robots.txt training token and page-level tags such as ai-grounding:disallow. After March 1, 2027, NOARCHIVE and NOCACHE are no longer read as training or grounding preferences, and pages without a temporary preference recorded in Bing Webmaster Tools default to Allow for grounding until Bing recrawls them.

What does DisallowExtended mean in Bing's AI grounding preference?

It's 1 of 3 values for Bing's page-level grounding preference. It keeps a page available to Microsoft-operated experiences such as AI Mode, AI Overview, Copilot, and Microsoft 365, and excludes grounding services Microsoft provides to other companies, such as Web IQ and the Foundry grounding API. Microsoft says choosing it reduces brand visibility in AI experiences.

Do I need to change robots.txt to allow AI training?

No. A file without a __usage_ai-training group expresses no preference, and User-Agent: * rules don't count as one. Microsoft treats both training and grounding as Allow by default. You can add User-Agent: __usage_ai-training with Allow: / to state it explicitly.
