Prompt Intelligence

How to find the AI prompts your brand shows up for (when Search Console only gives you impressions)

SEO teams want the AI equivalent of a keyword report: the prompts where ChatGPT, Gemini, or Google AI Overviews mention them. No engine publishes one. Search Console's generative AI report shows impressions by page, with no queries. AI Mode queries are mixed into the standard Performance report, where very rare queries are withheld. Bing shows sampled grounding queries for Copilot, and ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity give site owners no prompt report at all. So you work in reverse: collect candidate questions from the data you do have, rewrite them the way buyers ask an assistant, and test them on the engines repeatedly. This guide covers the filters, the sources, and a 5-step method, with what each source can and can't tell you as of Oct. 14, 2026.

Direct answer

You can't download a list of the AI prompts your brand appears in, because no engine publishes one. Search Console's generative AI report shows how often your pages appeared in AI Overviews and AI Mode, with no queries. Bing Webmaster Tools shows a sample of the search phrases Copilot used to find your pages. ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity offer site owners no prompt report.

So work in reverse. Collect candidate questions from what you can see: long and question-form queries in Search Console, Bing's grounding queries, and the questions your sales and support teams already hear. Rewrite them the way a buyer would ask an assistant. Then run them on the engines your buyers use, several times each, and record whether the answers name you, recommend you, or cite your pages. The prompts you keep become your prompt set: the fixed list of questions you measure over time.

Why there is no AI keyword report

A keyword report works because search queries are short and repeated. Millions of people type the same 3 words, so a ranking for that string is a stable fact you can report.

Prompts don't behave that way. Google says the average AI Mode search is triple the length of a traditional search (May 19, 2026), and that follow-up queries in AI Mode grew by more than 40% a month on average in the U.S. In a UC Berkeley study of 24,069 conversations with search-enabled AI models, prompts averaged 57.1 words. Long, specific questions are rarely asked twice in the same words.

That has 2 consequences. The engines have little reason to report prompt strings, and so far none do for site owners. And when a prompt does reach a report, it is often too rare to show: Search Console's Performance report documentation says that "to protect user privacy," it might not track "some queries that are made a very small number of times."

Step 1: Mine Search Console for conversational queries

Google confirms that AI Mode activity is in the standard Performance report. Its counting rules say a follow-up question in AI Mode is "essentially performing a new query," and John Mueller said in August 2026 that Search Console "includes information on AI Overviews and AI Mode in the general performance report," when asked about rows such as "yes, pricing" in the queries table. There is no filter that separates them, but 3 regex filters on the Query dimension narrow the list to likely candidates. Search Console uses RE2 syntax; the (?i) prefix makes a pattern ignore case.

  • Read them by page. Add the Page dimension and compare with the pages that show impressions in the generative AI report. A long question-form query landing on a page with AI impressions is a strong candidate.
  • Expect a floor, not a census. Rare queries are withheld, so most real prompts never appear. What you find is the visible fraction.
  • Watch for automated probes. Monitoring tools send scheduled prompts to Google too. Oddly specific long queries with steady impressions and no clicks may be machines checking a brand, not buyers.
  • Don't over-read a match. A 12-word question can be an ordinary search. The filters find candidates; testing in Step 4 decides what matters.

Question form(?i)^(what|how|which|why|is|can|should|does|i)\bQueries that start like a question. Google lists What, How, I, Is, and Can as the top first words in AI Mode queries, with "which" growing fastest

Follow-ups(?i)^(yes|yeah|ok|okay|sure|tell me more|go on)\bShort replies that only make sense inside an AI conversation

Step 2: Read Bing's grounding queries

Bing Webmaster Tools' AI Performance report shows grounding queries, which Microsoft describes as "the key phrases the AI used when retrieving content that was referenced in AI-generated answers." They are the AI's search, not the user's prompt, and Microsoft says they represent "a sample of overall citation activity." Still, they are the only first-party list of query phrasings tied to AI citations of your pages.

On Oct. 13, 2026, Microsoft added Topics Portfolio (Beta), which "groups the grounding queries where your site appears into broader thematic clusters" and lists Top Opportunity Queries: "the strongest queries across your whole portfolio where other sites appear and yours does not." Microsoft shows some volumes in the new view as ranges and asks you to treat them "as directional signals for planning, not as a precise ledger."

Use both lists as raw material. A grounding query such as "payroll software contractor payments multiple countries" points to the buyer question behind it, and an opportunity query names a topic where you are absent.

Step 3: Add the questions you already hear

The richest source of prompts isn't a search tool. It's the questions buyers already put to your team, in roughly the words they would type into an assistant.

  • Sales calls and demos: the comparison and "will it work for us" questions asked before a deal.
  • Support tickets and onboarding: the setup, integration, and limit questions people ask after buying, which other buyers ask AI before buying.
  • Lost-deal and closed-won interviews: what the buyer was looking for, and which alternatives they considered.
  • Public communities: the threads where your buyers ask each other for recommendations, and the reviews they leave.

Step 4: Rewrite, then test on the engines

Turn each candidate into a full question with the context a buyer would give: role, company size, constraint, and what they are comparing. Keep a mix of types: category questions asked before the buyer knows your name, comparison and use-case questions, and a few branded questions to check accuracy. Run each prompt more than once

Run every candidate on the engines your buyers use, and run it several times. Answers vary from run to run, so one check is one sample (see how many runs an AI-visibility number needs). For each answer, record whether it names you, whether it recommends you, which brands it names instead, and which pages it cites.

Keep the prompts with real buyer intent, including the ones where you never appear. A prompt at zero with real demand is the most useful gap in the set, not a prompt to delete (why). Once the set is fixed, read it per prompt rather than as one average (how).

Step 5: Keep the set current

New questions appear when a market changes: a product launch, a pricing change, a news story, a new integration. Repeat Steps 1 to 3 monthly and add the new candidates. When you add or retire a prompt, start its own history, so the trend for the rest of the set isn't distorted.

What to skip

Four shortcuts look tempting and mislead.

  • Asking an assistant which prompts it mentions you for. It has no access to other people's conversations, so its answer is a guess.
  • Treating "AI prompt volume" as observed data. No engine publishes prompt-level volume, so any volume figure for a prompt is modeled from other signals. That can be useful for prioritizing, as long as the method is disclosed.
  • Tracking only branded prompts. They almost always name you and tell you little about discovery.
  • Deciding from one run. A single answer is one draw; read a prompt over several runs.

What may change

These reports are moving quickly, so date anything you write down. Google added a multimodal search-type filter to the generative AI report on Sept. 24, 2026, and Bing's topic view arrived on Oct. 13. In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority's interpretative notes on its publisher conduct requirement (June 3, 2026) expect Google to give publishers click-through data from links in its AI features, "fully disaggregated from other elements of general search," through a platform such as Search Console. Query-level data is not on that list, so plan as if the prompt itself will stay hidden.

FAQ

Can I see which ChatGPT prompts mention my brand?

Not from OpenAI. ChatGPT offers site owners no prompt or citation report; you see it only as referral visits, which carry utm_source=chatgpt.com. To learn which questions mention you, write the prompts your buyers ask and run them on ChatGPT repeatedly.

Does Search Console show AI Mode queries?

Mixed in with everything else. Google counts AI Mode queries, including follow-ups, in the standard Performance report under the Web search type, with no filter that separates them. The generative AI performance report shows impressions by page only, with no queries.

What regex finds AI-style queries in Search Console?

Three filters on the Query dimension narrow the list: question openers such as (?i)^(what|how|which|why|is|can|should|does|i)\b, queries of 10 or more words with ^(?:\S+\s+){9,}\S+$, and short follow-ups such as (?i)^(yes|ok|sure|tell me more)\b. They find candidates, not confirmed AI queries, and very rare queries are withheld.

What are grounding queries in Bing Webmaster Tools?

The search phrases Microsoft's AI used to retrieve your pages when it cited them in Copilot or Bing's AI summaries. They are a sample, and they are the AI's search rather than the user's prompt, but they are the only first-party list of query phrasings tied to AI citations of your pages.

Is AI prompt volume data real?

No engine publishes how often a prompt is asked, so any prompt volume figure is an estimate built from other signals, such as search demand for the underlying question and community activity. Use it to prioritize, and ask how it was modeled.
