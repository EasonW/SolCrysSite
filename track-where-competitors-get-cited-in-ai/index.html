Direct answer

To track where competitors get cited in AI search, run a 4-step process: (1) build a 10–25-prompt competitive set covering the questions buyers ask before choosing a vendor; (2) run it repeatedly on the engines your buyers use, which for most B2B teams means ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews / AI Mode, Gemini, and Perplexity, plus Claude for technical buyers; (3) capture which competitors were mentioned, which got the primary (first or recommended) slot, which URLs were cited, and which cited source each mention appears to come from; (4) calculate competitive share of voice and the citation gap per prompt and per engine. The output tells you which competitor wins which prompt on which engine, which third-party sources put them there, and where your brand is being substituted out.

Why competitor AI citation tracking matters

A B2B buyer asks ChatGPT something like "what's the best cloud data warehouse for a mid-market analytics team?" The answer typically names a handful of vendors, often with a one-line description of each and a recommendation. If your brand isn't on that list, you aren't a tab the buyer opens. Your competitor is.

This is a different failure from losing a Google ranking. On Google, you can see you're on page 2, and the buyer might still find you. In an AI answer, the answer is the shortlist: if you're not in it, you didn't lose the click, you lost the consideration. Tracking which competitors appear in your place is the most direct way to see who is quietly inheriting that pipeline.

The research says buyers really do shortlist this way, and also that they check. In G2's April 2026 survey, 51% of B2B software buyers said they start research in an AI chatbot more often than in Google, and 69% said they chose a different software vendor than planned based on AI chatbot guidance. Gartner found in May 2026 that 45% of B2B buyers used GenAI mainly to gather vendor information, and 69% validate AI output with sales reps. And in G2's July 2026 buyer report, review sites (38%) narrowly edged out AI chatbots (37%) as the top shortlist influence. The competitive surface is the answer plus the sources behind it.

Competitor citation tracking is how you find out (a) who leads that surface in your category, (b) which specific prompts they own, and (c) which third-party sources are amplifying them. Without that data, AEO work is opinion. With it, the work has a target.

The 4-step competitive tracking process

Each step feeds the next: the prompt set defines the market you measure, the engines define where you measure it, the fields define what you can learn, and the metrics turn it into a ranking you can act on.

Step 1: Build a competitive prompt set

The prompts that matter for competitive tracking differ from the ones you use for brand monitoring. Brand monitoring leans on prompts where the buyer already knows your name ("is [your brand] any good?"). Competitive tracking leans on prompts where the buyer doesn't know any names yet and asks the engine to produce a shortlist.

A good competitive set has 10–25 prompts across four buckets. For a 22-prompt set, a reasonable split is 8 category-discovery, 6 use-case, 4 versus, and 4 replacement prompts. Our golden prompt set methodology covers sourcing them from sales-call transcripts rather than guessing:

  • Category-discovery prompts: "what are the best [category] tools in 2026?" or "top 5 [category] vendors compared." These produce the default shortlist your category cycles through.
  • Use-case prompts: "best [category] tool for [persona] working on [job]." These split the shortlist by buyer segment and reveal niche winners.
  • Versus prompts: "X vs. Y for [job]." These show how the engine describes trade-offs and which features it leans on.
  • Replacement prompts: "alternatives to [competitor]" or "is there a cheaper option than [competitor]?" These are where you have the best chance of inserting your brand against a dominant competitor.

Step 2: Run the prompts across the engines that matter

For most B2B teams in 2026, minimum coverage is ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews / AI Mode, Gemini, and Perplexity. Add Claude for developer and technical buyers, and retail assistants such as Alexa for Shopping for consumer categories.

Each engine retrieves differently and produces a different shortlist, so measure them separately:

  • ChatGPT search: per OpenAI, it uses third-party search providers and partner content, and OpenAI's own OAI-SearchBot surfaces sites in its search results (GPTBot is the separate training crawler). Since August 2026 it has cited forums far less and official vendor pages more.
  • Perplexity: real-time retrieval with explicit citations attached to most claims, which makes it the easiest engine to reverse-engineer.
  • Google AI Overviews / AI Mode: built on Google's Search index and core ranking systems with query fan-out, so it is the closest to traditional SEO.
  • Gemini: grounded in Google Search, yet it often produces a different shortlist than AI Overviews despite the shared infrastructure.
  • Claude: answers from its own web search; Anthropic doesn't document the provider (it is reported to be Brave Search), and in our measurement Claude has never cited Reddit.

Step 3: Capture the right fields per response

Run each prompt on each engine repeatedly, on a rolling daily or weekly cadence, not once. Answers vary from run to run: SparkToro's January 2026 study of 2,961 runs found less than a 1-in-100 chance that two runs return the same brand list, and in our own repeated runs, the set of sources cited for the same prompt changes from day to day. A single run is a snapshot; 20 runs is a distribution, and competitive decisions belong in the distribution.

For every prompt × engine response, capture at least four fields. The fourth is the most under-collected and the most valuable, because it turns "competitor X was mentioned a lot" into "competitor X was mentioned a lot because one analyst roundup is cited in 14 of our 22 prompts." The first is a vanity number; the second is an action:

  • Brand mentions: every competitor named anywhere in the response, including in cited sources.
  • Primary mention: which competitor was named first or explicitly recommended ("the best choice is X"). Being listed and being recommended move differently, so record both.
  • Cited URLs: every link the engine used to ground the response, mapped to its domain and to whether that domain belongs to a competitor, an editorial outlet, a community site (Reddit, Quora, Stack Exchange), or your own brand.
  • Source of mention: for each competitor mention, which cited URL the engine appears to have drawn it from.

Step 4: Calculate competitive share of voice and citation gap

Once the data is in, two metrics matter most, and both should be cut per prompt and per engine before anyone looks at the blended number.

Competitive share of voice (CSOV) is your share of all brand mentions across the tracked set: CSOV = (your brand mentions) / (your brand mentions + all tracked competitors' mentions) × 100, over a fixed prompt set and time window. It is the cleanest single-number competitive ranking. The formula variants (mention-rate, primary-mention, and citation-rate) are covered in how to track AI share of voice.

Citation gap is the difference between how often a competitor is cited as a source (its own domain appears in the engine's citation list) and how often it is mentioned in the answer. A competitor with a small citation footprint but a high mention rate is winning on third-party amplification: editorial, review sites, and communities. A competitor with a large citation footprint and a low mention rate is winning on owned content but losing on positioning. The two patterns call for different counter-strategies.

A more advanced metric, competitor replacement rate, measures how often a competitor is named on a prompt where your brand could plausibly be the answer; it is defined below.

How to read the board

  • Snowflake and Databricks are the default shortlist. Each appears in more than half of responses, so any prompt where the buyer hasn't named a vendor will probably include them. Displacing them on category-discovery prompts takes either editorial amplification (being cited in the same roundups and review pages they are) or a use-case position where their fit is weaker.
  • BigQuery and Redshift form a second tier. They appear in roughly a quarter to two-fifths of responses, the band where a few points of movement is the difference between considered and not considered.
  • Northwind appears in 14% of responses and is recommended in 2%. The gap between those two numbers is the diagnosis. Its move is not to chase Snowflake on "best data warehouse" prompts, a multi-year fight, but to win the prompts where it has a real edge: a specific workload, a deployment model, or buyers replacing an incumbent.

What the analyst does next

Three moves turn the board into a plan. Done well, it becomes a quarterly one-pager that product marketing uses to prioritize content, demand gen uses to prioritize PR, and product uses to spot positioning drift.

  • Pull the per-prompt breakdown. A 61% category-level mention rate hides which prompts each leader owns. Snowflake might dominate category-discovery prompts while Databricks wins the AI-workload prompts; the action plan differs.
  • Pull the citation source map for each leader. If a leader is cited mainly through one analyst roundup, one review-site category page, and one community thread, those are three outreach targets. If it is cited across 40 outlets, that is a structural moat and calls for a different strategy.
  • Identify the replacement prompts. Which prompts name a top-2 competitor but could plausibly include your brand? That is the highest-return prompt list, because the answer is already shaped as "recommend a vendor"; you need to be on the list.

Three high-value competitive insights you can extract

Once the data is flowing, three insights repay the analyst effort most.

1. Competitor citation source map

For each competitor, list every third-party domain cited alongside it in the last 30 days, ranked by frequency, and split by engine. This is a literal map of the competitor's PR, community, and editorial footprint as the engines see it.

The result is often a surprise. We have seen competitors whose mentions trace mostly to one community thread and one review-site category page, and others whose footprint spans 30 or more outlets. The first is fragile: one moderation decision, or one engine deciding to stop citing a platform, as ChatGPT did with Reddit in August 2026, can flip it. The second is structurally entrenched and needs a multi-quarter response.

It is also the most actionable insight, because it tells you where to invest your own outreach, guest essays, podcast appearances, and analyst briefings.

2. Competitor replacement rate

Competitor replacement rate is the percentage of prompts your brand is positioned for on which a given competitor is named. Calculate it as (prompts where competitor C is mentioned and your brand is positioned for the prompt) / (total prompts your brand is positioned for) × 100.

It tells you who is taking your place, not in the abstract but on the specific prompts your buyers ask. If competitor X has a 60% replacement rate and competitor Y has 20%, X is the brand you build comparison content against. The term is not standardized across tools; some call it prompt overlap, competitive mention rate, or substitution index. The calculation matters more than the label.

3. Competitor positioning in answers

This is the qualitative layer: for each prompt where a competitor is mentioned, capture how the engine describes it, then track the ratio across the prompt set. A competitor described positively on 80% of category-discovery prompts has won the narrative; one described with qualifications ("good but lacks...") 60% of the time has a gap you can compete on. Sort each mention into one of three buckets:

  • Positive or aspirational: "the leading platform for X."
  • Neutral or categorical: "options include X, Y, and Z."
  • Negative or qualified: "X is expensive" or "X is good but lacks Y."

Common mistakes in competitive AI tracking

Tracking too few or too many competitors. Track a core set of 5–8. Fewer than 5 under-represents the category and can overstate your own share; more than 8 dilutes the signal, as long-tail brands drift in and out of answers from week to week. If your category has more, keep a watch list outside the headline denominator. This matches the guidance in how to track AI share of voice.

Confusing brand mention with primary mention. Being named fourth in a list of five is not the same as being the recommended pick. Always separate "mentioned anywhere" from "named first or recommended"; the two move differently and call for different actions. More in AI share of recommendation.

Ignoring the source layer. "Competitor X has 60% share of voice" is a dashboard number. "Competitor X has 60% share of voice because one analyst roundup, one review-site category page, and one trade-press list are cited in 14 of our 22 prompts" is an action plan. Teams that stop at the first number often run a content sprint on their own pages and see no change, because the answer is being held in place by sources they don't own. The citation gap audit is built to surface that layer.

Reacting to a single bad run. Answers vary between runs, so one run where you lost to a competitor is a data point, not a trend. Make decisions on a rolling 14–30-day distribution, and be wary of any dashboard that only shows the latest run.

Blending engines into one number. A single cross-engine figure hides the moves that matter. In August 2026, ChatGPT all but stopped citing Reddit while Google AI Overviews did not (Search Engine Journal has the details); a competitor with a Reddit-heavy footprint would have lost ChatGPT citations and kept Google's, and a blended number would have averaged that away. Track the engines your buyers actually use, each on its own.

Using the map to attack a competitor. Competitive data is for understanding the map, not for manipulating a competitor's reputation. Don't seed negative reviews, brigade communities, or pose as buyers. Beyond the ethics and legal exposure, Google's spam policies now cover attempts to manipulate generative AI responses. Work on what you publish about your own brand and on earning genuine third-party coverage.

FAQ

How many competitors should I track?

A core set of 5–8. Fewer than 5 under-represents the category and can overstate your own share of voice; more than 8 dilutes the signal as long-tail brands drift in and out of answers. If your category has more real competitors, track the extras on a watch list outside the headline calculation.

Can I see what AI says about my competitor specifically?

Yes, if your tool stores the full response text rather than only counts. Then you can read the exact sentences an engine uses to describe each competitor, prompt by prompt, alongside the URLs it cited. That text is where positioning insights come from; a mention count alone won't show you how a competitor is framed.

What if my competitor doesn't have a well-known brand name?

Two paths: (1) track it by domain as well as by name, since an engine may cite a company's site without naming it prominently; and (2) track it by product name or founder name. Smaller competitors are often referenced through a single product page or a founder's profile rather than a category-level brand mention.

What's the difference between share of voice and citation share?

Share of voice is about mentions in the answer text: how often a brand is named relative to the tracked set. Citation share is about which URLs the engine points to as sources: how often a brand's own domain appears in the source list. A brand can have high share of voice and low citation share (it is named because editorial outlets and review sites talk about it) or the reverse (its documentation is cited, but the answer doesn't recommend it). Track both.

How often should I re-run competitive tracking?

Run the prompts daily, with repeated runs per prompt per engine; review the per-prompt and per-engine cuts weekly; and re-baseline the prompt set quarterly. Daily, because answers vary from run to run; weekly, because that is the cadence at which a team can act on a finding; quarterly, because buyer language and the category itself shift.

Does this work in a new category where AI answers are still forming?

Yes, with a caveat. In a young category, answers often lean on the few outlets and communities that have covered it, so your tracking will partly measure which sources are shaping the narrative. That is still useful: it tells you whom to brief and where to earn coverage early.

Is competitive tracking allowed under each engine's terms?

Running your own prompts and reading the answers is ordinary use. Automated, high-volume querying is governed by each engine's terms, and measurement methods differ: API capture, capture of the consumer interface, and search-results capture for Google AI Overviews, which has no public API. Each has trade-offs, because a consumer surface can behave differently from its API. Ask any vendor how it captures each engine and whether it discloses method changes; our visibility measurement methodology describes ours.
