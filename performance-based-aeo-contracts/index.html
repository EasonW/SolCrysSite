Performance-based AEO contracts: 5 questions to ask before you sign

Some answer engine optimization (AEO) agencies now offer performance-based contracts: fees begin only after AI assistants name your brand in an agreed share of prompts, or the agency takes a share of the revenue attributed to AI. Moving the risk onto the agency sounds like the safest way to buy AEO, and it can be. But the risk does not disappear. It moves into the definitions: which prompts count, how demand is weighted, what the baseline was, what counts as visible, and how revenue is attributed. This guide gives the 5 questions that test those definitions, a worked example of a threshold that is met while buyer prompts do not move, and the clauses worth writing into the contract.

Direct answer

A performance-based AEO contract ties what you pay an agency to an outcome in AI answers. It comes in 2 shapes. In a visibility-threshold deal, fees begin once AI assistants name your brand in an agreed share of an agreed list of prompts. In a revenue-share deal, the agency takes a percentage of the customers or revenue attributed to AI.

Both can be fair. Neither is safe by default, because the outcome is only as meaningful as its definitions. Before signing, get written answers to 5 questions: who picks the prompts; whether they are weighted by real buyer demand; what the baseline and the noise are; whether "visible" means named, recommended, or accurate; and, in a revenue-share deal, how incremental revenue is attributed.

If the answers are written into the contract, you have a real performance deal. If they stay verbal, you have a retainer with a delayed start date.

What a performance-based AEO contract actually pays for

Paying for outcomes is not new. Pay-for-performance search engine optimization (SEO) deals tied fees to rankings for years, and Google's hiring guidance for SEOs still says, "No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google" (Do You Need an SEO?). A familiar complaint about those deals was ranking first for keywords nobody searched.

The AEO version replaces a ranking with a rate: the share of prompts in which ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or Google AI Overviews names the brand. A rate over repeated prompts is a legitimate metric. SparkToro's study of 2,961 repeated runs concluded that a visibility percentage across many prompts, each run multiple times, is reasonable to track, while ranking position in an AI answer is not (SparkToro).

So the open question is never whether the rate can be measured. It is who controls the inputs. The table shows what each of the 2 performance models, and a flat fee for comparison, leaves the buyer to check.

Contract modelWhat triggers the feeWhat the buyer has to verify
Visibility thresholdAI assistants name the brand in an agreed share of an agreed prompt listThe prompt list, the demand behind it, the baseline, and what counts as a mention (questions 1–4)
Revenue shareCustomers or revenue attributed to AIThe attribution method and what would have happened anyway (question 5)
Flat retainer or subscriptionTime or access, regardless of outcomeThat the reporting holds up; questions 1–4 apply to any AEO report, with or without a performance term

Question 1: Who picks the prompts, and can the list change?

The metric is a share of a list, so whoever writes the list sets the difficulty. Answers to branded prompts such as "What is [Brand]?" usually name the brand before any work is done. Long-tail prompts that few people ask are far easier to win, often with a page written to match them. Category prompts, the ones a buyer asks before they know your name, are where the competition is and where a mention is worth something.

Ask for 4 things in writing. The full prompt list is attached to the contract, with each prompt labeled branded, long-tail, or category. Branded prompts are reported separately and kept out of the threshold. The threshold has to be met inside the category bucket, not only on the blended number. And the list is frozen for the term: a replacement needs written agreement and starts its own baseline. We publish our own prompt-selection method so buyers can check it before they sign (Golden Prompt Set Methodology).

Illustrative scenario only. A mid-market data-warehouse vendor signs a contract with a 40% threshold on 30 agreed prompts. Snowflake, Databricks, and BigQuery dominate the answers to category prompts such as "best cloud data warehouse for mid-market analytics." Three months later the blended rate is 53%, the threshold is met, and fees begin. The table shows where the 53% came from.

Prompt bucketBrand namedWhat it tells you
Branded (12 prompts)96%Already true on day 1
Long-tail, low demand (10 prompts)40%Won with pages written to match the list
Category buyer prompts (8 prompts)5%Unchanged; the category leaders still own the answer
Blended (30 prompts)53%Threshold met

Question 2: Are the prompts weighted by real buyer demand?

An unweighted share counts a prompt nobody asks the same as the one your buyers ask every week. Freezing the list is not enough if the list was built from questions with no demand behind them.

Answer engines do not publish prompt-level volume, so the demand behind a prompt has to be estimated from other signals. That makes the evidence behind each prompt the thing to ask for.

Ask the agency to show, for each prompt, where the demand signal comes from: search demand for the underlying question, questions asked in public communities, and the questions your own sales and support teams hear. Then ask for demand tiers (high, medium, and low), and for a threshold that has to be met inside the top tier. As a cross-check, Prompt Pulse is our public dataset of buyer-style questions by industry, each scored with a modeled, relative demand tier (how it is built).

Question 3: What is the baseline window, and how big is the noise?

AI answers are sampled, not looked up. In SparkToro's 2,961 runs, the same prompt returned the same list of brands less than 1 time in 100. A threshold only means something if the distance between baseline and target is larger than what the engines produce on their own.

The arithmetic is simple enough to check yourself. Thirty prompts run once on 4 engines is 120 answers. A measured rate of 40% on 120 answers has a 95% margin of error of roughly ±9 points from sampling alone, so a move from 33% to 40% is inside the noise. Run the same set daily for a week and the 840 answers bring the margin to about ±3 points. The published sample-size evidence is in our guide to how many runs an AI-visibility number needs.

That estimate covers sampling only. Engines also change what they cite without notice: in the week of Aug. 10, 2026, ChatGPT all but stopped citing Reddit (Search Engine Journal). A change of that kind can move a brand's rate with no work by anyone. So measure the noise floor from the baseline itself, as the week-to-week spread of the rate before any work starts.

Ask for a baseline measured before work begins, over several weeks of repeated runs, on the same prompts and engines that will judge the threshold. Ask that the threshold be sustained over a stated window, such as 4 consecutive weeks, not reached on a single best run. Ask for 2 or 3 competitors to be tracked on the same prompts as a control: if every brand moves together, the engine moved, not the agency. And ask who runs the measurement. If it is the agency's own tool, you need the raw answers and cited sources for every run, or a second measurement the agency does not control.

Question 4: Does "visible" mean named, recommended, or accurate?

A named-in rate counts any answer that contains the brand's name. That includes an answer that lists you as the budget option and recommends a competitor, and an answer that quotes a price you retired last year.

The gap between appearing and being chosen is measurable. In Google AI Overviews, SEO analyst Lily Ray found that when a brand's own "best [category]" listicle was cited, the brand was left out of the recommendation in 224 of 323 cases (Lily Ray). Accuracy is the third level: in G2's 2026 survey of 1,076 B2B decision-makers, 64% said they encounter inaccuracies in AI chatbot answers often or very often (G2).

We cover each level separately: cited but not recommended, the AI Recommendation Score, and whether AI is telling the truth about your brand. Ask which of the 3 levels in the table triggers the fee, and ask for all 3 to be reported separately. At minimum, an answer that names you unfavorably or states something false about you should not count toward the threshold.

LevelWhat countsWhat a fee tied to it rewards
NamedThe brand's name appears anywhere in the answerPresence, including unfavorable or wrong mentions
RecommendedThe answer positions the brand as a pick for the buyer's needBeing chosen, which is what moves a shortlist
AccurateWhat the answer says about the brand matches the brand's own factsBeing described correctly on pricing, capabilities, and limits

Question 5: How is incremental revenue attributed?

A revenue-share deal is only as good as its attribution, and the standard reports do not connect an AI answer to a sale. Google Analytics 4's AI Assistant channel excludes Google AI Overviews and AI Mode. Search Console's generative AI report shows impressions only. Bing Webmaster Tools' AI Performance report shows citations without clicks. Our guide to what AEO does to your web traffic covers what each report shows and misses.

Much of the influence does not click. A buyer reads an AI answer, then arrives days later through a branded search or a direct visit, and last-click analytics files the visit under organic or direct. A contract that pays on "revenue attributed to AI" has to say which of those visits count.

The harder half is what would have happened anyway. Revenue grows for reasons that have nothing to do with AI answers: seasonality, pricing changes, paid campaigns, a product launch, and ordinary SEO. Without an agreed baseline trend or a control group, a revenue share can pay the agency for growth it did not cause.

For a revenue-share deal, write these into the contract:

  • Which sessions count as AI-referred, as a named referrer list, and whether branded-search and direct visits count at all.
  • The baseline trend those visits are compared against, measured before the engagement starts.
  • The lookback window between an AI-referred visit and a sale.
  • What is excluded: existing customers, pipeline already open at signing, and revenue from campaigns the agency did not run.
  • Who owns the analytics property and the raw data, and a cap on the total share.

Clauses worth writing into the contract

The 5 questions turn into contract language. If one of these clauses is missing, the performance term depends on trusting the party that gets paid when it is met.

Add 2 clauses the questions do not cover. The first protects the brand rather than the budget. A fee tied to a number rewards the cheapest way to move that number. Google's spam policies have covered "attempting to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search" since May 15, 2026, and its generative AI guide says seeking inauthentic mentions across the web "isn't as helpful as it might seem." The consequences land on your domain, not the agency's. List the tactics that are off the table, require disclosure of any paid placement, and keep approval over anything published in your name (which tactics cross the line). The second is the exit: decide at signing who keeps the content and the measurement history when the contract ends.

The table puts all 9 clauses in one place.

ClauseWhat to put in writing
Prompt listThe full list as an exhibit, each prompt labeled branded, long-tail, or category; branded prompts kept out of the threshold; the list frozen for the term
DemandThe demand evidence for each prompt, demand tiers, and a threshold that must be met in the top tier
BaselineMeasured before work starts, over several weeks of repeated runs, on the same prompts and engines
ThresholdMet inside the category bucket, larger than the baseline's own week-to-week spread, and sustained for a stated window, with competitors tracked as a control
Counting ruleWhether named, recommended, or accurate triggers the fee; unfavorable and false mentions excluded
MeasurementWho runs it, and access to the raw answers and cited sources for every run
AttributionReferrer list, baseline trend, lookback window, exclusions, data ownership, and a cap (revenue-share deals)
TacticsProhibited tactics, disclosure of paid placements, and approval rights over published content
ExitWho owns the content and the measurement history when the contract ends

FAQ

Is pay-for-performance AEO a red flag?

Not by itself. Tying fees to outcomes aligns incentives when the outcome is defined tightly. It becomes a red flag when the agency chooses the prompt list, measures the result with its own tool, and counts any mention as a win. Ask the 5 questions and get the answers into the contract.

Can an AEO agency guarantee AI visibility?

No. No agency controls what an answer engine says: answers are sampled, sources change, and models update on the engines' own schedule. A performance-based contract is a contingent fee, not a guarantee, and it is only as strong as its definitions. Treat a promise of a specific result as a claim to test with the 5 questions.

What is a fair AI visibility threshold?

There is no universal number. A fair threshold is set on non-branded prompts with demand evidence, sits above the measured baseline by more than the baseline's own week-to-week spread, and has to hold for several consecutive weeks. A threshold that includes branded prompts, or that can be met on a single run, is not a performance term.

Should branded prompts count toward the threshold?

No. AI assistants usually name a brand when the question is about that brand, so branded prompts inflate the rate from day 1. Track them separately for accuracy, where they matter most, and keep them out of the threshold.

Can revenue from AI answers be attributed accurately?

Only partly. Visits from AI assistants can be counted where a referrer is passed, but Google Analytics 4's AI Assistant channel excludes Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, and much of the influence arrives later as a branded search or a direct visit. A revenue-share contract needs an agreed referrer list, baseline trend, lookback window, and exclusions. Without them the number is a negotiation, not a measurement.

Does SolCrys guarantee results?

No. We commit to a published prompt-selection method, a prompt set that changes only with your approval, reruns on the same prompts and engines, and separate readings for mention, recommendation, and accuracy. Outcomes also depend on third-party sources, engine updates, and competitors, which no vendor controls.
